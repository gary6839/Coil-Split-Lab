Inside the push-pull pot

How the switch and the coil split are wired

A push-pull pot is two parts in one: an ordinary pot and a separate switch underneath it. They only work together if you wire them that way. Many push-pull pots, CTS among them, carry a small switch board labeled C1, 1, 2 and C2, 3, 4. C1 and C2 are the commons; each one connects to one of its two neighbors depending on the knob position.

Split part

Note: The pot works like any standard pot. The switch board isn't connected to it; it's just the contacts for two switches that flip together when you push or pull the knob.

What you see in the cavity

What it means electrically

Reading the board

  • Pushed down: C1 connects to 2, and C2 connects to 4.
  • Pulled up: C1 connects to 1, and C2 connects to 3.
  • For a basic split: the series link wires go on C1, and pad 1 goes to ground with a short jumper to the pot casing. A resistor or cap for a partial split goes in that jumper. Pad 2 stays empty, so pushing the knob down leaves the link wires floating.
  • A common mistake, even in some videos and diagrams: link on C1 with ground on pad 3. That never splits, because pad 3 belongs to C2. Before you solder up, check with a meter: knob pulled up, you should get continuity from the series link wires to ground; pushed down, none.
  • The second side (C2, 3, 4) is free. It can split a second pickup, flip phase, or switch the coils between series and parallel.
  • Check the labels printed on your board. Some boards also have a second set of pads laid out like a standard push-pull switch, and switches without a board have unlabeled lugs: the middle row is the commons.

A separate switch works the same way

A DPDT mini toggle has the same six connections: two rows of three, with the commons in the middle row. Wire it exactly like C1, 1, 2 and your pot stays an ordinary pot.

The push-pull simply puts the switch under a knob so there are no new holes in the guitar. The trade-offs: a mini toggle shows its position at a glance, while a push-pull is invisible and easy to bump.

Getting rid of 60 Hz hum

A humbucker's two coils pick up the string in phase but pick up hum out of phase, so the hum cancels. Any split that takes one coil out at 60 Hz brings the hum back.

On the graph: the dot at 60 Hz shows the hum, drawn as if the hum field were as strong as the string. The gap down from the curve is the hum rejection: on a single coil the dot sits on the curve, on a humbucker it drops far below. Green is quiet (more than 15 dB below one coil alone), yellow is some hum (6 to 15 dB), red is hum (less than 6 dB). Tick Show hum curve to see the hum at every frequency, including the buzz harmonics a cap split lets through.

  • Hard short gives the full single-coil hum.
  • A resistor leaves part of the second coil working, so part of the cancelling survives. Higher values keep more.
  • A capacitor barely conducts at 60 Hz, so the coils keep cancelling hum while the top end splits. Buzz harmonics above the split point are not cancelled.
  • Raising the pickup lifts the signal without lifting the hum, which helps a split position.

Which noise do I have?

  • Touch the strings or the jack. If the buzz drops, it's electrostatic room buzz. Shielding and good grounding fix it.
  • Turn the guitar toward and away from the amp or computer. If the hum rises and falls, it's magnetic. Only humbucking fixes it, or a split that keeps the coils cancelling at 60 Hz.
  • Shielding can't stop magnetic hum. At 60 Hz, copper only blocks a magnetic field when it's several millimeters thick. Foil blocks electric fields, and that's its job.

Grounding the shield: connect it to ground at one point, for example one wire from the foil to the ground bus that joins the pot backs. Overlap the foil pieces so they touch, and make sure the cover plate's foil touches the cavity foil. Several separate ground connections can form loops that add noise; a shield that isn't grounded at all does nothing.

How this model works

Each coil is a resistance and an inductance, and the two coils are magnetically coupled (coupling factor 0.3, an estimate). The split part sits across the coil that turns off. The load is the volume and tone pots, the tone cap, the cable, a 1 MΩ amp input and about 120 pF of pickup capacitance. Shielding adds about 30 pF for cavity foil and about 150 pF with shielded wire runs (estimates; an LCR meter from hot to the shield, pickups disconnected, gives the real number).

Hum is modeled as an equal outside field through both coils, cancelling to within 5% on a matched pair. Mismatched coils, as in a hybrid, cancel less. Coil turns are estimated from resistance.

The model has no eddy-current losses, so resonances (especially the cap-split bump) may look sharper than a real pickup's. Values marked as estimates should be replaced with measured ones. The model will be checked against a breadboard before it goes into the main simulator.
